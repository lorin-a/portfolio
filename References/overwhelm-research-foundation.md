# Overwhelm: Research Foundation for Whelm

A working evidence base for the thesis that overwhelm is the experience produced when expectations exceed needs (or, more precisely, when perceived demands exceed perceived capacity to meet them). Compiled from ten sources of varying weight, ranging from peer-reviewed psychology to popular self-help.

---

## 1. Lorin’s thesis

Overwhelm stems from a conflict between needs and expectations. In Whelm’s framing, the inner state of being human — what a body, nervous system, and life actually need to function — is in tension with the load of expectations placed on it (from self, others, work, culture). When the gap widens past a tipping point, the result is the felt experience we call overwhelm.

## 2. Verdict

**Largely supported, with one important refinement.** The dominant academic framing across these sources is that overwhelm is a perceived imbalance between **demands and resources** (or **load and capacity**) — a near-cousin of Lorin’s “expectations vs needs.” The Frontiers 2025 study, currently the strongest peer-reviewed source on the lived experience of overwhelm, explicitly names **expectation violation** (failing to meet internal or external standards) as one of the core appraisals that trigger overwhelm. Brené Brown converges on the same point via Kabat-Zinn (life “unfolding faster than the human nervous system and psyche are able to manage well”). The thesis is **strongest** when framed as *perceived demand or expectation exceeding perceived capacity or what the nervous system needs*; it is **weakest** when stated as a clean two-term equation, because the literature is clear that the mechanism is appraisal-driven, cumulative, and entangled with control, unpredictability, and self-judgment — not just a simple ledger.

## 3. Source-by-source findings

### 3.1 Dael, Meister, Krings (2025) — *Frontiers in Organizational Psychology*

**Title:** “Reaching point break: understanding the lived experience of being overwhelmed”
**Authors:** Nele Dael, Alyson Meister, Franciska Krings
**URL:** https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/organizational-psychology/articles/10.3389/forgp.2025.1590159/full
**Context:** Peer-reviewed qualitative study (Sept 29, 2025). Strongest academic anchor in this set. Treats overwhelm as a distinct affective state rather than a synonym for stress or burnout.

Verbatim:

- (Findings, formal definition) “a distinct affective state occurring at the perceived tipping point when situational demands are appraised as exceeding available resources, and is marked by heightened alertness yet bodily fatigue, limited outward expression, withdrawal or mental freeze, and the conscious experience of predominantly negative feelings” ([frontiersin.org](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/organizational-psychology/articles/10.3389/forgp.2025.1590159/full)) — **directly supports the thesis.**
- (Method, framing of stress) “the transactional theory of stress…defines stress as an adaptive response to a perceived imbalance between situational demands and personal coping resources” ([frontiersin.org](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/organizational-psychology/articles/10.3389/forgp.2025.1590159/full)) — **directly supports the thesis.**
- (Findings, on expectation violations) participants described “value judgments of self or others that heightened the situation’s perceived demands,” including beliefs that “they were violating or failing to meet their own *internal* standards (e.g., regarding professionalism, productivity, output quality, or even morality and values)” ([frontiersin.org](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/organizational-psychology/articles/10.3389/forgp.2025.1590159/full)) — **directly supports the thesis, with the refinement that the gap is partly self-generated.**
- (Findings, tipping point) “Several participants literally referred to a well demarcated point in their experience that elicited overwhelm,” e.g. “the wifi company not honouring their appointment was *the tipping point* on a lot of stress I was already holding onto” ([frontiersin.org](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/organizational-psychology/articles/10.3389/forgp.2025.1590159/full)).
- (Method, role overload definition) “role overload: situations in which work demands are high and resource availability is low” ([frontiersin.org](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/organizational-psychology/articles/10.3389/forgp.2025.1590159/full)).

Theoretical scaffolding cited in the paper: Lazarus & Folkman (1984) transactional theory; Bakker & Demerouti (2007) Job Demands-Resources; Hobfoll (1989, 2011, 2018) Conservation of Resources; Demerouti et al. (2001) on burnout ([frontiersin.org](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/organizational-psychology/articles/10.3389/forgp.2025.1590159/full)).

### 3.2 Owens (2022) — OHSU Center for Women’s Health

**Author:** Sarah Owens, M.S.W., LCSW, counselor at OHSU Center for Women’s Health
**URL:** https://www.ohsu.edu/womens-health/new-definition-overwhelm
**Context:** Practitioner-authored institutional piece for an academic medical center. Not peer-reviewed, but clinically grounded and frames overwhelm in cumulative, expectation-laden terms.

Verbatim:

- “My patients are coming in saying, ‘I can’t take one more stressor.’” ([ohsu.edu](https://www.ohsu.edu/womens-health/new-definition-overwhelm)).
- “What may seem like a small issue is actually sitting on top of a much larger pile of stressors.” ([ohsu.edu](https://www.ohsu.edu/womens-health/new-definition-overwhelm)) — **supports the cumulative-load reading of the thesis.**
- “The danger is that setting too many expectations can quickly lead to feeling overwhelmed, and suddenly crawling back under the covers seems the better option.” ([ohsu.edu](https://www.ohsu.edu/womens-health/new-definition-overwhelm)) — **the clearest direct statement of expectations → overwhelm in this source set.**
- “There is a lot of grief going on, a feeling of loss of what we were once able to do daily, to have, and to share.” ([ohsu.edu](https://www.ohsu.edu/womens-health/new-definition-overwhelm)).

### 3.3 NIMH — *So Stressed Out* fact sheet

**Publisher:** National Institute of Mental Health (NIH Publication No. 20-MH-8125)
**URL:** https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/so-stressed-out-fact-sheet
**Context:** US government health authority, written for a general audience. High credibility, low conceptual depth.

Verbatim:

- “Stress is the physical or mental response to an external cause, such as having a lot of homework or having an illness.” ([nimh.nih.gov](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/so-stressed-out-fact-sheet)).
- “Anxiety is your body’s reaction to stress and can occur even if there is no current threat.” ([nimh.nih.gov](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/so-stressed-out-fact-sheet)).
- Stress becomes risk territory when symptoms “interfere with your everyday life,” “cause you to avoid doing things,” or “seem to be always present” ([nimh.nih.gov](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/so-stressed-out-fact-sheet)).

Note: NIMH does **not** use needs/expectations language explicitly. Its frame is symptom-and-function, not appraisal. Useful as a credibility anchor for talking about stress mechanisms but not for the thesis itself.

### 3.4 Brown (2024) — brenebrown.com

**Author:** Brené Brown, PhD, LMSW. Researcher and bestselling author.
**URL:** https://brenebrown.com/articles/2024/11/21/stressed-and-overwhelmed/
**Context:** Popular essay drawn from *Atlas of the Heart* (2022). Brown is influential but not peer-reviewed; her originality here is the synthesis. She cites peer-reviewed sources (Cohen et al. 1983, Gohm 2003, Kabat-Zinn 2019, Lindquist et al. 2015).

Verbatim:

- “For me, stress is barely managing the Whac-A-Mole game at the carnival, and overwhelm is leaving the carnival in tears. And, not being able to find my car.” ([brenebrown.com](https://brenebrown.com/articles/2024/11/21/stressed-and-overwhelmed/)).
- “We feel stressed when we evaluate environmental demand as beyond our ability to cope successfully. This includes elements of unpredictability, uncontrollability, and feeling overloaded.” ([brenebrown.com](https://brenebrown.com/articles/2024/11/21/stressed-and-overwhelmed/)) — **supports the thesis** (demand vs ability to cope).
- (Quoting Jon Kabat-Zinn) “Overwhelm is the all-too-common feeling that ‘our lives are somehow unfolding faster than the human nervous system and psyche are able to manage well.’ Our bodies and minds are experiencing that quicksand feeling.” ([brenebrown.com](https://brenebrown.com/articles/2024/11/21/stressed-and-overwhelmed/)) — **the most quotable line in this entire set for Lorin’s thesis.**
- “There is growing evidence that the antidote to overwhelm appears to be nothingness or ‘non-doing’ time as Kabat-Zinn describes it.” ([brenebrown.com](https://brenebrown.com/articles/2024/11/21/stressed-and-overwhelmed/)).
- “The big learning here is that feeling both stressed and overwhelmed is about our narrative of emotional and mental depletion — there’s just too much going on to manage effectively.” ([brenebrown.com](https://brenebrown.com/articles/2024/11/21/stressed-and-overwhelmed/)).
- (Citing Carol Gohm) “overwhelmed” describes “an experience where our emotions are intense, our focus on them is moderate, and our clarity about exactly what we’re feeling is low.” ([brenebrown.com](https://brenebrown.com/articles/2024/11/21/stressed-and-overwhelmed/)).

### 3.5 Lertzman (2012) — Climate Access

**Author:** Dr. Renee Lertzman, environmental psychologist
**URL:** https://climateaccess.org/blog/psychology-overhwelm-why-understanding-it-first-step-addressing-it/
**Context:** Practitioner blog in the climate-communications space. Not academic, but psychoanalytically literate; reframes overwhelm as a defense mechanism, which is a useful counterweight to demand-resource framing.

Verbatim:

- “Feeling overwhelmed relates to the experience of being submerged, inundated. But it’s arguably more of a symptom than a ‘thing,’ or a ‘barrier.’” ([climateaccess.org](https://climateaccess.org/blog/psychology-overhwelm-why-understanding-it-first-step-addressing-it/)) — **partial challenge to the thesis** (treats overwhelm as downstream of something else).
- “Defense mechanisms, put simply, are strategies we engage individually and socially to manage anxiety, distress, loss and related undesirable experiences.” ([climateaccess.org](https://climateaccess.org/blog/psychology-overhwelm-why-understanding-it-first-step-addressing-it/)).
- “If we track overwhelm we may find it leads us into unforeseen territories, such as fearing loss of control, anxiety over low self-efficacy, or intense sadness.” ([climateaccess.org](https://climateaccess.org/blog/psychology-overhwelm-why-understanding-it-first-step-addressing-it/)).
- (Citing Harold Searles) “The current state of ecological deterioration is such as to evoke in us largely unconscious anxieties…” ([climateaccess.org](https://climateaccess.org/blog/psychology-overhwelm-why-understanding-it-first-step-addressing-it/)).

### 3.6 Petrie (2014) — *Wake Up!* white paper, Center for Creative Leadership

**Author:** Nick Petrie, CCL
**URL:** https://www.ccl.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/04/WakeUp.pdf
**Context:** Industry white paper from a major leadership-development org. Draws on Dr. Derek Roger’s 30-year research program on stress and rumination. Useful for the pressure-vs-stress distinction, but methodologically light.

Verbatim:

- (p. 3, Root Cause) “the major factor that determines your stress levels is not what exists ‘out there’ in the environment, but what is happening ‘in here’ in your thinking. Your boss is not stressful; your reaction to him/her is.” ([ccl.org](https://www.ccl.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/04/WakeUp.pdf)).
- (p. 3) “Pressure is the external demand in the environment. Everyone has pressure in their work and life: deadlines, projects, family demands. That is not stress. Stress is what people do with that pressure in their minds.” ([ccl.org](https://www.ccl.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/04/WakeUp.pdf)) — **complicates the thesis**: external load alone does not produce overwhelm; the appraisal/rumination layer does.
- (p. 3) “Rumination is the mental process of thinking over and over about something, which happened either in the past or could happen in the future, and attaching negative emotion to it.” ([ccl.org](https://www.ccl.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/04/WakeUp.pdf)).
- (p. 6) “Some demand in your environment can help motivate you to perform. Just don’t let that demand turn into rumination.” ([ccl.org](https://www.ccl.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/04/WakeUp.pdf)).

### 3.7 Kabigting (2019) — *Nursing Science Quarterly*

**Title:** “Conceptual Foreknowings: Integrative Review of Feeling Overwhelmed”
**Author:** Edwin-Nikko R. Kabigting, RN, MS
**URL:** https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0894318418807931
**Context:** Peer-reviewed integrative literature review across nursing, business, psychology, philosophy, theology, social work, education, and sociology. **Full text is paywalled** — only the abstract is available.

Verbatim (abstract):

- “Feeling overwhelmed is a universal human universe living experience of living quality.” ([journals.sagepub.com](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0894318418807931)).
- “Three themes that emerged from the extant literature were (a) feeling overwhelmed arises as an engulfing turbulence, (b) feeling overwhelmed surfaces with disquiet isolation, and (c) feeling overwhelmed emerges with reaching for relief.” ([journals.sagepub.com](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0894318418807931)).

The phenomenological framing here (turbulence, isolation, reaching for relief) is compatible with the thesis but does not directly name expectations or needs. Note: full text would need to be obtained through a library to mine further.

**Author:** Emily Guarnotta, PsyD. Medical reviewer: Rajy Abulhosn, MD.

**Context:** SEO-driven clinical-information site, written by a licensed psychologist. Practitioner consensus, not research.

### 3.9 Bowers (2026) — STACK Model white paper

**Author:** Mark Bowers, PhD
**URL:** https://www.drmarkbowers.org/stack-model-white-paper
**Context:** Author-developed framework for children/adolescents. Not peer-reviewed; useful for its load/capacity vocabulary, which maps almost one-to-one onto Lorin’s thesis.

Verbatim:

- “A load-based capacity framework for understanding behavioral escalation in children and adolescents.” ([drmarkbowers.org](https://www.drmarkbowers.org/stack-model-white-paper)).
- “Observable behavioral shifts occur when accumulated multi-domain demands exceed available regulatory capacity.” ([drmarkbowers.org](https://www.drmarkbowers.org/stack-model-white-paper)) — **directly supports the thesis.**
- “When cumulative load crosses a Capacity Threshold, behavior shifts from flexible engagement to Protection Mode.” ([drmarkbowers.org](https://www.drmarkbowers.org/stack-model-white-paper)).
- “Load refers to the total regulatory demand placed on the nervous system across interacting domains.” ([drmarkbowers.org](https://www.drmarkbowers.org/stack-model-white-paper)).
- “Regulatory capacity refers to the available neurocognitive and autonomic resources required for executive functioning, emotional modulation, behavioral inhibition, social responsiveness.” ([drmarkbowers.org](https://www.drmarkbowers.org/stack-model-white-paper)).
- “Load is additive and context-dependent.” ([drmarkbowers.org](https://www.drmarkbowers.org/stack-model-white-paper)).

## 4. Convergent themes

1. **Overwhelm = perceived demands exceeding perceived resources (or load exceeding capacity).** Dael et al. (2025), Brown (2024) via Lazarus, Bowers (2026), Owens (2022), Guarnotta (2024). This is the dominant framing.
2. **Appraisal is doing the work, not raw load.** It’s the *evaluation* that demand exceeds capacity that produces overwhelm, not the demand alone. Dael et al. (2025), Brown (2024), Petrie (2014).
3. **A tipping point separates stress from overwhelm.** Stress is manageable juggling; overwhelm is the point where the system breaks. Dael et al. (2025) “tipping point,” Brown (2024) “leaving the carnival in tears,” Bowers (2026) “Capacity Threshold,” Owens (2022) “one more stressor.”
4. **Cumulative, multi-domain load — not single events.** Stressors stack across domains and time until a small final input triggers the state. Owens (2022), Bowers (2026), Dael et al. (2025).
5. **Expectation violation is a distinct trigger.** Failing to meet internal or external standards heightens perceived demand. Dael et al. (2025), Guarnotta (2024) “high expectations and low support,” Owens (2022) “setting too many expectations.”
6. **Control and unpredictability amplify it.** Overwhelm is worse when the load feels uncontrollable or unforeseeable. Brown (2024), Kanojia on Robbins (2024), Lertzman (2012).
7. **Cognitive consequences: flooding, freeze, narrowing.** Once in overwhelm, decision-making, planning, and emotional clarity degrade. Brown (2024) “poor decision making,” Dael et al. (2025) “withdrawal or mental freeze,” Nerurkar on Robbins (2024) “psychological flooding,” Gohm via Brown “clarity about exactly what we’re feeling is low.”
8. **Antidote is non-doing / nervous system regulation, not more effort.** Brown (2024) “non-doing,” Petrie (2014) “wake up,” Choosing Therapy (2024) grounding/sensory breaks.

## 6. Gaps and cautions

- **The thesis as stated is slightly underspecified.** “Needs vs expectations” is intuitive but the literature uses *demands vs resources* or *load vs capacity*. The closest direct match for “expectations” in the academic source is Dael et al.’s “normative significance” and “violating internal/external standards.” Consider tightening the language: *overwhelm is what happens when the gap between what we’re expected to handle and what our nervous system can actually carry passes a tipping point.* That keeps Lorin’s framing while landing inside the empirical literature.
- **Appraisal is not optional.** Petrie/CCL (and the transactional theory underneath Brown and Dael) insist that pressure isn’t stress and load isn’t overwhelm until a person *evaluates* it as exceeding their capacity. A skeptical reader will push back on any framing that implies overwhelm is purely environmental. The thesis is strongest when it includes the appraisal step.
- **Brené Brown and Mel Robbins are popular, not peer-reviewed.** Brown’s essay is solid because it cites real research (Kabat-Zinn, Gohm, Cohen, Lindquist), but Brown herself shouldn’t be the academic spine — quote her for resonance, cite Kabat-Zinn and Dael et al. for credibility. Robbins is colour only; if she appears in the case study at all, treat her as media context, not evidence.
- **The Kabigting paper would strengthen the foundation.** It is the closest existing integrative review of feeling overwhelmed as a concept and it is **paywalled** here. If Lorin wants to lean on a nursing/phenomenology angle, she should pull the full PDF through a library; the abstract alone isn’t enough to cite confidently.
- **NIMH does not say what Lorin needs it to say.** The fact sheet defines stress symptomatically, not as a needs/expectations conflict. Cite it for the existence and prevalence of stress, not for the thesis mechanism.
- **Lertzman runs against the grain.** Her psychoanalytic frame — overwhelm as a *defense* covering deeper anxieties about control, self-efficacy, and loss — is a useful counterweight worth acknowledging. The case study will be stronger if it nods to this view rather than pretending the demand-resource model is the only one.
- **No source in this set directly tests Whelm’s exact wording (“needs vs expectations”).** The closest empirical work uses adjacent constructs. The thesis is **supported by analogy and adjacent evidence**, not by a paper that operationalizes the exact two-term equation. That’s honest to say in the case study.
- **Cultural / class blind spots.** All ten sources frame overwhelm at the individual or family level. None of them seriously interrogate structural causes (precarity, caregiving asymmetry, racialized labour, climate). A reader from a critical-theory background will notice the omission. Worth deciding upfront whether Whelm wants to stay inside the individual-psychology frame or extend it.
