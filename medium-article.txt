According to the U.S. Surgeon General’s Framework for Workplace Mental Health & Well-Being, 76% of U.S. workers have experienced at least one symptom of a mental health condition. Additionally, 84% said that their work conditions have caused mental health challenges. As a result, 81% of workers are now prioritizing workplaces that offer mental health support when looking for future jobs. In healthcare, where workers devote themselves to caring for others, the impact is even more profound — over 1 in 5 healthcare workers in the U.S. have experienced PTSD (1). These facts highlight the need for workplaces to show greater compassion and care for the mental well-being of their employees, especially those who dedicate their lives to helping others.\n\nMental health challenges in healthcare settings often stem from the inherently “caring” nature of the work combined with the burden of excessive administrative and clerical tasks.\n\nMental health challenges in healthcare settings often arise from the deeply emotional and compassionate nature of the work. Healthcare professionals frequently provide care for individuals dealing with severe or life-altering issues, such as depression, grief, and bereavement. They may support patients experiencing the loss of a loved one, including the devastating experience of illness or death of a child. This constant exposure to intense emotions and difficult situations takes a significant emotional toll, as caregivers empathize deeply with their patients. Coupled with heavy workloads, demanding schedules, and long hours, the strain can lead to burnout, compassion fatigue, and other mental health challenges (1).\nMoreover, during our class session with Heidi Donovan and Grace Campbell, healthcare workers often face the burden of excessive administrative and clerical tasks, such as assisting patients into screening rooms, coordinating with insurance providers, and managing medical reports. These tasks can feel misaligned with the patient-centered focus of their training, leaving some feeling disconnected from the purpose that initially drew them to the profession. This added workload amplifies stress and reduces the time and energy available for direct patient care or self-care, further impacting their mental well-being. Reducing these burdens is essential to fostering a more supportive environment for healthcare professionals.\n\nAddressing mental health issues in the workplace can be achieved through both top-down and peer-to-peer approaches\n\nAddressing mental health issues in the workplace can be effectively supported through top-down approaches led by organizational leadership. Visible, accessible, and transparent management practices foster a supportive environment, while clear communication of policies that prioritize employee well-being is essential (1). Providing psychoeducation and therapy resources is particularly impactful — 86% of participants in a study on healthcare worker stress during COVID indicated that prior training would have been beneficial (1). Training supervisors to detect burnout, promote work-life balance, and create psychologically safe spaces further enhances workplace mental health (1). Additionally, fostering inclusivity through training on bias, diversity, equity, and inclusion (DEI) helps build a safer and more welcoming environment for all employees.\nPeer-to-peer initiatives are equally important in addressing workplace mental health. Recognizing and celebrating staff contributions fosters a sense of belonging and support, whether from colleagues, doctors, or even patients and caregivers (1). Programs that encourage peer-to-peer recognition, such as support groups or acknowledgment initiatives, create opportunities for staff to connect and share experiences. By promoting mutual understanding and appreciation, peer-based approaches complement leadership efforts, creating a workplace culture that prioritizes mental well-being.\n\nThe stigma surrounding mental health issues has hindered the acceptance and adoption of mental health care.\n\nThe stigma around mental health makes it harder for people to accept and seek mental health care, especially in the workplace. For example, 73 percent of emergency physicians feel there is a stigma about mental health treatment in their jobs (1). This stigma causes fear, with 57 percent of these physicians worrying that getting help for their mental health could affect their careers (1). Even more concerning, 27 percent have avoided seeking treatment altogether because they are afraid of professional consequences (1). These numbers show how important it is to break the stigma and create workplaces where people feel safe and supported in getting the help they need.\nPress enter or click to view image in full size\n(Source)\nStakeholder Analysis\nTeam Expertise Assessment\n\nElijah Benzon (he/him)\n\nI am currently a graduate student at Carnegie Mellon University studying Interaction Design. First, cast a wide net — observe, ask questions, and gather diverse perspectives. Then, refine findings into actionable insights. This approach prevents premature conclusions and ensures research is both expansive and focused.\n\nFly-on-the-Wall Observation — To capture genuine behavior, minimize your presence and let people act naturally. Observe interactions, movements, and pain points without interfering. This method is invaluable in settings where direct questioning might alter behavior, like workplaces or public spaces.\n\nWalk-a-Mile Immersion — Gain firsthand experience by stepping into your users’ shoes. Whether shadowing a nurse, navigating a city with mobility restrictions, or working within limited resources, this method builds empathy and uncovers challenges that traditional research might miss.\n\nProject Timeline\nPress enter or click to view image in full size\nDeliverables\n\nDeliverables will be dependent on our research findings and the problems we identify, however, we do enjoy entertaining ideas such as:\n\nImproving workflows for better communication between staff and upper management.\nPress enter or click to view image in full size\nTaken on 4th floor @ Magee, we were told it is under utilized and has poor placement\n\n2. In a profit-driven, hierarchical system that treats staff as disposable, the intense focus on patients comes at the cost of worker support, leading to burnout, poor recognition, and a toxic workplace culture. This imbalance ultimately undermines the quality of care for both patients and providers, highlighting the urgent need for a model that values healthcare workers as essential to sustainable, high-quality care.\n\nHere is a breakdown of the our key themes that support this hypothesis:\n\nSystemic Issues\n\nA major challenge in healthcare workplaces is the disconnect between staff and leadership. Many employees feel unheard due to a lack of formal feedback systems, which can lead to mismanagement and frustration. Power dynamics between roles, generational differences, and varying levels of engagement among staff further contribute to workplace tensions. Addressing these issues requires better communication, stronger leadership involvement, and a culture where staff feel comfortable voicing concerns.\n\nDisconnect Between Staff and Leadership\nStaff often feel unheard due to the lack of feedback systems. This can lead to frustration and burnout. For our project, it shows the need to improve communication between staff and leadership.\nRecommendation: There is a need for a better communication method that is accessible for both parties. A method where staff can openly and anonymously share their concerns, which is then translated to statistics and informative data for administrators.\nPower Dynamics Between Roles\nThe differences in authority between nurses, doctors, and admin staff can make working together difficult. This creates stress and impacts teamwork. Our project needs to create more equal communication across roles.\nRecommendation: Staff bonding events such as an activity similar to a ropes course, which centers on the value of teamwork. Perhaps an activity where staff members switch roles.\nGenerational Tension\nYounger staff and older leaders often clash due to different working styles. This tension adds to stress and can lead to burnout. Our project should help bridge this gap.\nRecommendation: Provide training on generational differences and set up mentoring programs to help staff work together better.\nPhysical Space/Environment\n\nThe design and condition of a workplace directly impact stress levels, efficiency, and overall well-being. In healthcare, where workers face constant pressure, an unwelcoming and impractical environment only adds to burnout. Overcrowded spaces, poor lighting, lack of privacy, and limited areas for rest and socialization make it harder for medical workers to recharge during their shifts. These environmental stressors contribute to exhaustion, lower morale, and decreased job satisfaction. Addressing these issues isn’t just about aesthetics — it’s about creating a space that supports the mental and physical well-being of healthcare workers, ultimately improving patient care.\n\nRecommendation: Use design principles and/or interior design skills to create the illusion of a bigger, more comfortable break room and quiet areas where staff can take a break and recharge.\nRecommendation: Set up private rooms or quiet spaces where staff can take a moment to decompress during their shifts. If there is no room, perhaps having a resource that teaches staff short, quick ways to hold space.\nRecommendation: Improve signage that is both informative and beautiful.\nLack of Benefits/Funding\nWhen there isn’t enough funding or support for healthcare workers, it leads to added stress and burnout. Workers face issues like parking disparities, paying for their own scrubs and equipment, and not having enough resources for professional growth. These gaps in support make staff feel undervalued and contribute to fatigue and frustration. Without more investment in both people and morale-boosting programs, it’s harder to retain staff and maintain a healthy work environment.\nRecommendation: Communicating the struggle of having to find parking and taking the shuttle to others.\nRecommendation: Having volunteer and donation opportunities for equipment for the nurses.
