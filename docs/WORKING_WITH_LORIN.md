# Working with Lorin — A Creative Portrait & Collaboration Guide

This document exists because strategy documents capture decisions but not the person behind them. If you're picking up this project, you need to understand Lorin — not just her portfolio requirements, but how she thinks, what she responds to, where she lights up, where she gets stuck, and what "right" feels like to her. Without this, you'll build something competent that feels generic. With it, you'll build something that could only be hers.

---

## WHO LORIN IS

Lorin is not a designer who learned to care about people. She's a person who has always cared deeply about people and found design as the discipline that finally held everything she already was. This distinction matters for everything you build with her.

She grew up between an engineer father who takes things apart to understand how they work and a special education teacher mother who listens to the words people use and what they reveal. This isn't biographical trivia — it's the literal origin of her design thesis. She inherited both impulses simultaneously and has spent her life looking for a container big enough to hold them together. Design is that container. Her portfolio needs to be, too.

She has lived many professional lives — journalism, marketing, caregiving, travel by foot and bicycle through countries she'd never been to — and each one sharpened a different tool she now brings to design work. She does not experience these as a scattered career path. She experiences them as accumulation. Every phase taught her something she now uses. A new collaborator who treats her pre-design work as "previous career" rather than "foundation" will lose her trust quickly.

---

## HOW SHE THINKS

Lorin thinks in **both/and**, never either/or. This is her superpower and the thing most people miss about her. She doesn't balance rigor and empathy — she doesn't experience them as opposing forces. To her, rigorous methodology IS an act of care. A carefully structured facilitation guide IS emotional labor. Data collection done right IS listening. When you present her with a binary choice (professional OR personal, minimal OR expressive, structured OR organic), she will resist it — not because she's indecisive, but because the binary itself is wrong. The right answer for Lorin almost always involves finding the version where both things are true at once.

She is a **systems thinker** who feels her way through systems. She can see the architecture of a problem — the stakeholders, the power dynamics, the feedback loops — but she navigates it through emotional intelligence, not just analysis. She reads rooms. She reads what people aren't saying. She calls this being "clairvoyant" and she's not entirely joking.

She processes ideas **iteratively and out loud**. She'll say something, hear herself say it, then refine it. Her first articulation of an idea is rarely her final one, but it's almost always directionally right. Don't dismiss her early phrasing — build on it. She'll sharpen it herself if you give her room.

She has a strong internal compass for **what feels right** even when she can't immediately articulate why. If something feels "basic" or "off" to her, trust that instinct. Push her to name what's wrong, but don't override the feeling. She's almost always detecting something real.

## HER CREATIVE TASTE

Lorin's aesthetic lives at the intersection of **warmth and sophistication.** She is drawn to things that feel:

- **Tactile and material** — stone, linen, dried flowers, handmade ceramics, embroidered textiles. Things with weight, texture, and history. Nothing glossy, nothing factory-new. Everything she loves has patina — evidence of being touched, chosen, weathered, collected.

- **Curated, not decorated** — her home is full of hand-dyed clothes, quilts her mom made, collected art, plants. Every object was chosen. This is the same eye she brings to design. She doesn't add things for visual interest; she selects things that belong. If something is present, it has a reason.

- **Organic but intentional** — she loves natural forms (ginkgo leaves, dried protea, river stones) but she arranges them with care. Her scanner scans of personal objects are the perfect example: wild, natural things placed deliberately on a dark field. Structure holding nature. Nature softening structure.

- **Rich but restrained** — she gravitates toward earth tones, muted jewel tones, and natural pigments. Her palette lives in sage green, plum, terracotta, chalcedony blue, cream, and amber — colors that come from her actual stones and flowers and childhood paintings, not a branding exercise. She prefers depth from texture and scale contrast, not from adding more colors.

- **Typography-forward** — she responds to sites where type IS the design, not decoration on top of imagery. Large, confident letterforms. Serif and sans-serif mixing. Text that has presence and weight on the page.

- **Calm but not boring** — she wants animations and interactions that feel like breathing, not like fireworks. Smooth easing, organic pace, scroll-driven reveals that reward attention. She will physically recoil from anything that feels hyperactive, flashy, or gimmicky. But she also gets bored by safe, expected design. The sweet spot is something that feels calm on the surface with surprises underneath for those who look closely.

### What she does NOT like:
- Corporate polish / SaaS marketing aesthetic
- Anything that performs personality loudly (declarations of quirkiness, forced humor)
- Generic minimalism that's actually just emptiness
- Overly trendy design that will feel dated in 18 months
- Anything that prioritizes looking impressive over being meaningful
- Split-screen metaphors or anything that argues her two sides are separate

### Sites she responded to strongly:
Olha Uzhykova (textured circles, shifting role labels, sage/cream palette), Flow (serif + sans mix, curving text), Renuka Gabhale (risograph grain, paper quality), Self Aware (deconstructed letterforms, personality in simplicity), Off Season (persistent sidebar context, oversized type, editorial metadata). Common thread: these sites feel like physical objects with weight and grain, not screens.

## HER PERSONAL OBJECTS AS DESIGN LANGUAGE

Lorin scanned her favorite things on a flatbed scanner — stones, dried flowers, an altar cloth with flower stains, childhood finger paintings, handmade earrings, embroidered textiles, ceramic drawer knobs, vintage pottery. These scans are not decoration. They are the Rosetta Stone for her entire visual identity.

**The color palette lives in these objects.** The sage of the aventurine. The blue of the chalcedony. The plum and purple of her childhood paintings. The terracotta of the altar cloth stains. The cream of the linen. Her design system colors should be *sampled from* these scans, not generated from theory.

**The texture language lives in these objects.** Linen weave. Stone grain. Embroidery thread. Pressed flower residue. Paper tooth from childhood art. These should inform background textures, hover states, section transitions — the material quality of the entire site.

**The emotional language lives in these objects.** These are things she kept, saved, pressed, preserved. They're evidence of someone who pays attention to what most people walk past. The site should feel like it was made by someone with this same quality of attention.

**The elemental framework maps to these objects without her trying.** Stones = Earth. Dried flowers = Air. Embroidered textile = Fire (someone's labor of love). Altar cloth with flower stains = Water (absorption, imprint, the record of something held). She literally curated the four elements instinctively.

When working with these scans: the dark-field (black background) scanner aesthetic gives each object its own gravity. They look like natural history specimen plates or Dutch still-life paintings. This quality — intimate, pressed-close, each thing getting to just BE — is core to her visual language.

## HER STRENGTHS (what the portfolio must demonstrate)

1. **She translates.** Between communities and institutions. Between feelings and frameworks. Between complexity and clarity. She takes what's invisible or unspeakable and gives it form. This is her rarest skill.

2. **She holds space.** She creates conditions where people feel safe enough to be honest. Her participatory research works because participants trust her. Her facilitation is trauma-informed not as a methodology checkbox but because she genuinely understands what it means to feel unsafe and what it takes to feel held.

3. **She distills.** She can take a sprawling, complex concept and find the single visual metaphor that makes it land immediately. Her poster work demonstrates this. So does her ability to synthesize research findings into actionable recommendations.

4. **She sees what isn't being said.** Pattern recognition across conversations, systems, and power dynamics. She notices the gap between what an institution says it values and what its systems actually do. This is the core of her systems thinking.

5. **She makes things real.** She's not just a researcher or strategist — she builds. Toolkits, brand systems, campaigns, prototypes, facilitation guides. She takes insight all the way to artifact.

6. **She has range without being scattered.** Photography, film, animation, poster design, participatory research, systems mapping, facilitation, brand strategy. Each skill feeds the others. The portfolio needs to frame this as accumulation, not diffusion.

7. **Her eye is extraordinary.** Her photography, her object curation, her scanner scans — she sees beauty and meaning in things most people walk past. This is the quality that makes her design work feel different from someone who just learned the methods.

---

## HER GROWTH EDGES (handle with honesty and care)

1. **She overshares.** She knows this about herself. She has so much she's proud of and so much context she wants to provide that she can overwhelm. The portfolio's job is to be her editor — to protect her strongest work from being diluted by volume. When she wants to add something, the right question is "does this strengthen or dilute what's already here?"

2. **She struggles with hierarchy.** She feels everything is important because it all connects. Helping her choose what goes first, what goes second, and what gets cut is a core part of the collaboration. Be direct about this. She responds well to honest editing.

3. **She can lose the thread between vision and execution.** She'll have a brilliant conceptual idea and want to jump to building before the structure is clear. Slow her down at these moments. Ask her to articulate what the visitor will actually experience before writing code.

4. **She sometimes conflates "meaningful to me" with "meaningful to the audience."** The scans, the personal objects, the childhood paintings — these are deeply meaningful to her. The challenge is making them meaningful to a stranger in 3 seconds. Always test ideas against "what does the hiring manager experience?" not just "what does this mean to Lorin?"

5. **She gets excited and wants to do everything at once.** Reign this in gently. The portfolio needs to ship. Scope creep is the biggest risk. A focused, polished three-case-study site beats an ambitious ten-feature site that's 70% done.

## HOW TO COLLABORATE WITH HER

### Do:
- **Ask before implementing.** She wants to be part of every decision. Surprising her with finished work she didn't approve will feel like a loss of control, not a gift.
- **Be a critical eye.** She explicitly wants a senior design director who pushes back. Don't just execute her ideas — challenge them. She responds well to "I hear what you want, and here's why I think this version is stronger."
- **Use her language.** She speaks in metaphor, in feeling, in sensory detail. "This section should feel like turning something over in your hands" is more useful direction to her than "add 16px padding."
- **Show her things she hasn't seen.** She said she wants to feel "unlimited yet grounded." Don't limit her worldview about what's possible. Bring references, techniques, and ideas she wouldn't have found herself — then help her evaluate what fits.
- **Tackle one thing at a time.** She prefers to fix/build incrementally rather than getting a massive changeset. This is her "pre-flight check" instinct. Honor it.
- **Protect her voice.** She insists on using her actual words, not paraphrased versions. When writing copy or describing her work, use her phrasing. She can tell when it's been smoothed into generic professional language and it bothers her.
- **Remind her of the audience.** When she's deep in the creative weeds, gently bring her back to: "What does the hiring manager need in this moment?" This recenters without dismissing her creative instincts.

### Don't:
- **Don't present binary choices.** She'll resist. Frame options as a spectrum or as "both, but here's how."
- **Don't use generic design language.** "Clean and modern" means nothing to her. Be specific. "The type is set at 72px Fraunces with 1.1 line height on a cream field with 120px top padding" — that she can react to.
- **Don't dismiss her pre-design work.** Her photography, animation, and poster work are not "previous career." They're the root system of everything she does now.
- **Don't over-explain what you're doing.** She's smart and she picks things up fast. Give her the essential "what and why," not a tutorial.
- **Don't let her skip the hard editorial decisions.** She'll want to include everything. Your job is to hold the line on focus while making her feel heard about what's being deferred, not deleted.

## THE FEELING THE PORTFOLIO MUST CREATE

If you build this right, a hiring manager will spend 90 seconds on the site and think:

*"This person is different. Not in a loud way — in a way I can feel but can't quite name. Everything here was chosen. The way the text moves, the texture behind the image, the way she talks about her participants. She clearly cares at a level that would elevate our team. And she can actually build things — this isn't just vision, it's execution. I want to meet her."*

That's the target. Everything you build, every decision you make, test it against that paragraph.
