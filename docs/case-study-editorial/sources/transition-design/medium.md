## Intro to Food Insecurity

Food insecurity is a pressing and systemic issue in Pittsburgh, where approximately 12.5% of the population struggles to access healthy, fresh, and nutritious food. This issue disproportionately affects vulnerable groups, including low-income individuals, the homeless, the elderly, single parents, children, and people with disabilities. Additionally, African American, Latino, and other marginalized communities face heightened risks due to systemic inequalities and historical patterns of discrimination, also known as “[food apartheid](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1bhG0pzTo8LRVscQzLGq7mHWm9aJr5uu3/view)”. Certain neighborhoods, such as the Hill District, Homewood, Clairton, and Larimer, are particularly affected, as these areas often lack full-service grocery stores or affordable fresh produce options. The combined effects of economic hardship, limited transportation, and inadequate food infrastructure leave many residents reliant on corner stores or fast food, perpetuating cycles of poor nutrition and health disparities. Addressing food insecurity in Pittsburgh requires a holistic understanding of these interconnected challenges to create equitable and sustainable solutions.

## Infrastructure, Technology, and Science

Food insecurity is often understood in terms of affordability or availability, but the underlying systems of infrastructure, technology, and science are just as critical to whether people can reliably access nutritious foods. In Pittsburgh and many urban centers with similar topography and aging facilities, a myriad of smaller infrastructural and technological gaps add up to create significant barriers to healthy food access.

**Infrastructure Limitations & Technological Gaps  
**Aging electric grids, weak water systems, and outdated roads all undercut the ability to grow, store, and distribute fresh food equitably. [Cold-chain failures](https://www.danfoss.com/en/about-danfoss/news/cf/insufficient-cold-chains-lead-to-food-loss-that-could-feed-950-million-people/) caused by inadequate refrigeration, power outages, or the high cost of cooling technology lead to excessive spoilage of perishable goods before they ever reach communities in need. Meanwhile, a [digital divide](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/opendata/mind-gap-enabling-data-smart-agriculture-all) in agriculture prevents small-scale and community-oriented producers from using data analytics to forecast demand, streamline logistics, or reduce waste. On top of that, equipment like automated tractors and greenhouse systems is prohibitively expensive, leaving smaller farms at an even greater disadvantage.

**Transportation & Grocery Placement  
**[Transportation bottlenecks](https://apps.pittsburghpa.gov/redtail/images/542_PRA_2016.pdf) (steep terrain, aging bridges, high transit fares) further restrict access to fresh food. For many low-income residents, having no personal vehicle or reliable public transit means traveling long distances to a supermarket is time-consuming or impossible. Complicating matters, “grocery deserts” result when large stores cluster in higher-income areas or suburbs, leaving neighborhoods with only corner stores that can’t stock fresh produce due to limited storage. Some areas, like Pittsburgh’s [Hill District](https://newsinteractive.post-gazette.com/food-deserts/), hadn’t had a full-service grocery in 30 years until 2013.

**Under-Resourced Food Processing & E-Commerce  
**Lastly, the lack of [smaller-scale food processing infrastructure](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9737221/) (canning, freezing, or dehydration facilities) means many local producers can’t preserve surplus. Without affordable e-commerce platforms and broadband coverage in underserved areas, online grocery sales don’t fill the gap. This combination of limited storage, costly transportation, poor digital access, and patchy grocery placement ultimately perpetuates a vicious cycle of high prices, restricted choices, and reliance on convenience foods — making nutritious diets unattainable for too many.

## Legal and Policy

The story of food insecurity in Pittsburgh is one of historical inequities, infrastructural limitations, and policy failures that combine to create a particularly challenging environment for vulnerable communities.

**Impact on Farmers**

The foundation of Pittsburgh’s food security challenges begins with the dramatic erosion of its local agricultural base. Since 2012, Allegheny County has lost nearly [6,000 acres of farmland](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1bhG0pzTo8LRVscQzLGq7mHWm9aJr5uu3/view) and 40 farms, largely due to suburban expansion and development pressures. This agricultural decline isn’t merely about numbers, it represents a fundamental shift in how the region feeds itself, forcing increased reliance on centralized big box stores and longer supply chains that make fresh food access more precarious for vulnerable populations.

Suburban development further exacerbates this issue, as developers purchase rural land for housing, decreasing the acreage available for farming. Discriminatory practices in federal land management and lending have historically limited farm ownership among people of color, [resulting in less than 2% of U.S. farmers being Black](https://www.policylink.org/food-systems/equitable-food-systems-resource-guide/production) and only 6.5% of private agricultural lands owned by non-White farmers. This disparity perpetuates food insecurity in minority communities by limiting access to fresh, locally grown produce.

Additionally, land grabbing by financial institutions for unsustainable monocropping and biofuel production threatens the availability of arable land for food cultivation. Federal policies, such as the [Farm Bill](https://www.policylink.org/food-systems/equitable-food-systems-resource-guide/production), favor commodity crops like corn, wheat, soy, and sugar through subsidies and crop insurance, leading to increased dependency on processed foods and limited access to fresh produce.

Urban agriculture initiatives, such as community gardens, face challenges due to complex zoning laws and lengthy application processes, deterring community efforts to enhance local food production. [Strict government regulations](https://www.fns.usda.gov/data-research/data-visualization/school-foodservice-supply-chain-challenges-dashboard) and complex certification requirements for food safety can inadvertently hinder small businesses and local farms from participating in institutional food programs like school lunches, limiting their ability to provide fresh, locally sourced food to schools and underserved communities.

**Existing Food Programs**

While [16.2% of Pittsburgh households](https://pittsburghpa.maps.arcgis.com/apps/dashboards/addd8498478b4b11b9212a37f31f0cf0) (22,811 as of 2020) receive SNAP benefits, the existing food assistance programs often perpetuate rather than solve the underlying problems. [Income eligibility thresholds](https://www.ers.usda.gov/data-products/charts-of-note/chart-detail?chartId=78787) exclude families who make too much to qualify but too little to afford healthy food. Even for those who do qualify, SNAP benefits frequently fall short of covering monthly food expenses, forcing recipients to rely on food pantries. The Thrifty Food Plan’s estimate of $52.70 for weekly food costs dramatically underestimates actual needs by about 60%.

Food distribution through food banks can be uneven due to unpredictable donations and supply issues, leading many individuals to also rely on soup kitchens. Stigma and accessibility challenges deter some from applying for government benefits, and SNAP programs often do not incentivize healthy eating, resulting in purchases of processed foods over more expensive organic produce.

Farmers markets in low-income communities face difficulties attracting vendors due to permitting complications and logistical challenges in accepting SNAP and Farmers Market Nutrition Program benefits. The charity model of food assistance, while addressing immediate needs, can create a cycle of dependency without tackling the root causes of food insecurity, failing to empower communities to develop sustainable, self-sufficient food systems. ([source](https://www.policylink.org/food-systems/equitable-food-systems-resource-guide/production))

**Policy and Regulations**

[Strict federal food donation rules](https://drive.google.com/file/d/113hxj_VHEKE0ULTeYuSFL2TlAv0m0Ct0/view) often prohibit perfectly edible items, discouraging supermarkets and other potential donors, resulting in fewer donations and less food available for those in need. In January 2016, the [Greater Pittsburgh Community Food Bank](https://pittsburghfoodbank.org/) and its partners served 68,365 individuals throughout the county.

Residents and small food entrepreneurs in food-insecure areas often face difficulty accessing capital for local food initiatives due to biased credit systems and [discriminatory lending practices](https://www.policylink.org/food-systems/equitable-food-systems-resource-guide/production), limiting the ability to fund small grocery stores, cooperatives, or urban farms. Racially discriminatory land use and zoning patterns contribute to Pittsburgh’s existing segregation issues, exacerbating food insecurity for minority residents.

Pennsylvania’s[ economic dependence on natural gas](https://insideclimatenews.org/news/17072022/pennsylvania-climate-change/) production creates a conflict between job creation and environmental sustainability, impacting food-insecure communities that have less autonomy in a top-down system.

## Social

Food insecurity in Pittsburgh represents a classic wicked problem, deeply rooted in historical injustices and perpetuated by complex, interconnected systems. The city’s struggle with food access is not merely about a lack of grocery stores, but a manifestation of decades of systemic racism, economic disparities, and urban planning decisions that have created a landscape of inequality.

Food insecurity in Pittsburgh is a major social determinant of health, with poor access to fresh food sources greatly contributing to the problem, particularly [impacting individuals from minoritized and low-income communities](http://d-scholarship.pitt.edu/44152/1/Cason,%20I%20MPH%20Essay%202023.pdf). This issue is not evenly distributed across the city; rather, it follows patterns of historical segregation and disinvestment. The Hill District, Homewood, and other predominantly African American neighborhoods bear a disproportionate burden of food insecurity, a direct legacy of practices like redlining and urban renewal projects of the 1960s and 1970s.

The average distance to a grocery store in food-insecure communities is 2.2 miles, increasing by about 1.1 miles for predominantly Black and Brown communities. This spatial inequality is compounded by transportation challenges, as households without personal vehicles are disproportionately Black and low-income, making reliable public transportation a necessity.

The systemic nature of food insecurity in Pittsburgh is evident in its far-reaching impacts. Older adults and people with disabilities are more likely to experience food insecurity, which in turn exacerbates chronic health conditions like diabetes, heart disease, depression, and anxiety. This creates a vicious cycle where food insecurity both causes and is caused by [poor health outcomes](https://www.pa.gov/en/ltgovernor/newsroom/in-visit-to-greater-pittsburgh-community-food-bank--lieutenant-g.html).

The problem has reached crisis levels, with the Greater Pittsburgh Community Food Bank reporting a significant increase in demand for food assistance. In September alone, they received [more than 1,800 calls for food assistance](https://pittsburghfoodbank.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/Report_Food-Insecurity-Hits-Record-High.pdf), and distributed 6% more food in the past three months compared to the same period last year. This surge in need reflects the compounding effects of economic pressures, rising food costs, and persistent systemic barriers.

The racial disparities in food insecurity are stark and reflect broader societal inequities. Black individuals are nearly 2.4 times more likely to face hunger than white individuals, while Latino individuals are 2.2 times more likely. These disparities underscore the deeply entrenched nature of food insecurity as a symptom of broader racial and economic injustices.

Addressing food insecurity in Pittsburgh requires a systems-thinking approach that recognizes the interconnectedness of various factors. Efforts to improve food security must go beyond simply increasing food availability. They must address the political, social, and logistical challenges that contribute to this wicked problem. This includes [developing collaborative methods](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11449854/), transparent data sharing, and governance structures aligned with the goal of improving outcomes for all residents.

Pittsburgh’s food insecurity crisis is not just about hunger; it’s a complex web of historical injustices, economic disparities, health inequities, and systemic barriers. Solving this wicked problem will require a holistic, multi-faceted approach that addresses not just food access, but the underlying systems of inequality that perpetuate food insecurity across generations and communities.

## Environmental

Food insecurity in Pittsburgh is not just a social issue, but a complex environmental challenge that intertwines with the city’s industrial past, present struggles, and future climate risks. This wicked problem creates a vicious cycle where environmental degradation exacerbates food insecurity, which in turn leads to practices that further harm the environment.

Allegheny County’s food system alone produces a staggering 3.7 million tons of carbon dioxide equivalent (CO2e) emissions annually, with each resident responsible for about 3.1 tons CO2e per year. This is equivalent to the emissions from driving 7,600 miles in a typical passenger vehicle, [surpassing the yearly emissions](https://www.cmu.edu/news/stories/archives/2020/april/greenhouse-gas-emissions-from-food-system.html) from powering all households in the county. These emissions stem from various sources, including food production, transportation, and waste.

The city generates an estimated[ 89,000 tons of food waste](https://apps.pittsburghpa.gov/redtail/images/14929_Food_Matters_Calculator_Summary_Pittsburgh_(1).pdf) each year, with restaurants being the highest contributors at 30,300 tons. This organic waste, comprising 28% of Pittsburgh’s landfill contents, not only takes up valuable space but also releases methane, a potent greenhouse gas.

The environmental impact of food insecurity extends beyond waste. [Food waste costs](https://www.pittsburghpa.gov/Business-Development/Sustainability/Food-Systems/Food-Waste) the country nearly $218 billion annually, and along with food, valuable resources like water and farmland are squandered when food is discarded. This wastage is particularly concerning given Pittsburgh’s industrial legacy. The food system itself is a major contributor to greenhouse gas emissions, producing between 19–29% of all GHG emissions globally. Agricultural production accounts for 80–86% of these emissions, with [additional GHGs coming from packaging](https://www.cmu.edu/epp/prospective/undergraduate/epp-project-courses/climate-change-and-the-pgh-urban-food-system_final_20200129.pdf), transportation, storage, and waste.

Pittsburgh’s struggle with food insecurity is deeply rooted in its environmental history. The widespread effects of climate change are intensifying and threatening the world’s food supply. Climate change impacts all four pillars of food security: availability, access, utilization, and stability. Future climate change is expected to affect food security through yield declines, increased prices, reduced nutrient quality, and [supply chain disruptions](https://www.cmu.edu/epp/prospective/undergraduate/epp-project-courses/climate-change-and-the-pgh-urban-food-system_final_20200129.pdf).

The city’s industrial past continues to haunt its present. Toxic industrial practices from steel production have eroded soil, water, and air quality, destroying biodiversity and creating environments unsuitable for safe produce growth. This contamination limits the viability of urban agriculture initiatives, potentially increasing reliance on imported food and exacerbating the area’s carbon footprint.

Climate change is further complicating Pittsburgh’s food security landscape. Rising temperatures and extreme weather events are disrupting natural ecosystems, altering soil moisture levels, and affecting plant growth and water availability. These changes not only threaten crop yields but also undermine biodiversity, as emerging pest populations and invasive species destabilize local habitats.

The scale of food waste in Pittsburgh is alarming, with up to 40% of food produced for human consumption going to waste. This waste represents not just lost food, but wasted water, energy, and emissions from every step in the food’s life cycle.

## Conclusion/Insights

**Systemic Barriers and the Fragmentation of Food Access**

Pittsburgh’s struggle with food insecurity is deeply intertwined with the structural and economic realities of the region. The fragmented nature of Allegheny County’s governance which comprises of numerous municipalities with varying priorities makes it difficult to implement large-scale solutions. This lack of coordination exacerbates the disconnect between rural farmers and urban consumers, limiting farmers’ ability to sell their goods locally while simultaneously restricting access to fresh food for food-insecure residents.

The city’s historic European-style planning and steep topography further complicate public transportation efforts. Residents in “food deserts” often face long, inefficient commutes to grocery stores, leaving them with few choices beyond corner stores and fast-food chains that lack nutritious options. The resulting diet-related health disparities disproportionately impact low-income and marginalized communities, reinforcing cycles of poor health and economic instability.

**Economic and Environmental Tensions**

Pennsylvania’s reliance on extractive industries, particularly natural gas production, reflects a broader economic tension between job creation and environmental sustainability. The prioritization of fossil fuel industries poisons local ecosystems while deepening the divide between policies that promote economic growth and those that ensure long-term ecological health. Meanwhile, Pittsburgh’s “eds and meds” economy continues to invest heavily in technological and medical innovation while neglecting more immediate infrastructural concerns, such as accessible public transit and pedestrian-friendly streets for those with mobility challenges.

These systemic imbalances extend into food policy, where strict nutrition regulations and corporate consolidation within the supply chain prevent locally grown produce from reaching schools and hospitals; institutions that should be at the forefront of promoting community health. The commodification of food under industrial agriculture has reinforced a market-driven ethos that distances people from their fundamental right to nourishment and their relationship with the land.

**The Erosion of Local Food Systems**

The dominance of corporate food giants, bolstered by globalization and policy structures that favor large-scale production, has made it nearly impossible for small farms and businesses to compete. Complex government regulations, logistical barriers, and an economic model designed to prioritize efficiency over community resilience leave little room for a thriving localized food economy. The perception that Pittsburgh’s environment has improved since the steel boom era fails to acknowledge persistent issues of poor air and water quality, ranking among the worst in the nation. Without aggressive policy intervention, deteriorating soil health and shrinking arable farmland will continue to pose significant challenges for a sustainable food system.

Grocery store mergers and corporate consolidation further erode consumer choice. Reduced competition allows chains to inflate prices without consequence, making it even harder for low-income communities to afford fresh, healthy food. Gentrification adds another layer to the crisis, displacing affordable food retailers that serve historically marginalized neighborhoods. The influx of wealthier residents drives up living costs, creating “food mirages” where healthy food is technically available but remains out of reach for long-term community members.

**The Fight for Food Sovereignty**

Food insecurity is often framed as a problem of affordability or supply, but at its core, it is a systemic issue of power and control. The infrastructures that dictate food access such as transportation networks, corporate supply chains, and land-use policies are deeply entrenched in economic and political forces that prioritize profit over people. Low-income communities face a constant battle for autonomy, where job and housing insecurity perpetuate cycles of stress and survival. Without systemic change, food will remain a commodity rather than a fundamental right, and Pittsburgh’s most vulnerable residents will continue to bear the weight of policies that fail to serve them. Culturally, food sovereignty means access to culturally relevant foods. Pittsburgh has a rich diversity of cultures whose health can be impacted if they are separated from traditional food ingredients of their heritage. Rampant colonization has severed the relationship between many communities and their land/food traditions which has a severe psycho-spiritual impact on overall health and wellbeing.

**The Social is the Political**

Food insecurity in the social sector is a deeply entrenched issue, and it comes as no surprise that systemic racial discrimination and gentrification are major contributors to the problem. However, what stands out is the immense societal pressure placed on vulnerable communities already navigating systemic inequalities. These communities are forced to make difficult decisions about securing sufficient and nutritious food for their families—something that should be a fundamental right, not a daily struggle.

This relentless battle for basic needs takes a significant toll on individuals and families, leading to chronic stress, anxiety, and other major health issues. The lack of access to affordable, healthy food perpetuates cycles of poor nutrition, which disproportionately impacts marginalized groups. Over time, this not only affects individual well-being but also stifles the growth and resilience of entire communities.

**The Voiceless Victim**

The environmental effects of food insecurity in Pittsburgh extend to local ecosystems and biodiversity. The city’s industrial legacy, particularly from steel production, has left a toxic imprint on much of the soil, limiting the viability of urban agriculture and potentially harming local flora and fauna. Climate change is further complicating the situation, with rising temperatures and extreme weather events disrupting natural ecosystems, altering soil moisture levels, and affecting plant growth and water availability. These changes threaten crop yields and undermine biodiversity, as emerging pest populations and invasive species destabilize local habitats. The urban heat island effect, exacerbated by food insecurity and limited green spaces, particularly impacts neighborhoods like the Hill District and Hazelwood, affecting both human comfort and urban wildlife.

# Transition Design: Food Insecurity in Pittsburgh — Mapping Stakeholder Relations

**Assignment #2: **Transition Design Seminar—Mapping Stakeholder Relations

## Introduction:

Food insecurity in Pittsburgh is not just about hunger, it is a reflection of deep systemic inequality. At the center of this crisis are the people with the least power: low-income families, elderly residents, frontline workers, and even local farmers, all struggling within a system that limits their access to basic necessities. Yet, on the other side, those with privilege such as corporations, real estate developers, and policymakers wield significant influence over how food is produced, distributed, and accessed. Some may be unaware of the crisis, others may feel it is not their responsibility, and some directly benefit from keeping things the way they are—whether through corporate profits, exploitative wages, or land acquisitions that further push vulnerable communities to the margins.

At the same time, a growing movement of advocates, researchers, and community leaders is working to challenge these inequities and build a more just food system. To create real change, we must first understand these competing forces; the powerless, the powerful, and those who seek to maintain the status quo. By recognizing the motivations, blind spots, and vested interests of each group, we can uncover key intervention points, forge unexpected alliances, and disrupt the cycles that sustain food insecurity.

## Process:

To better understand the complexity of the problem, we analyzed key stakeholders who influence the food system, categorizing them into four groups:

- Those most affected

- Those in positions of power

- Those with the ability to create change

- Those who benefit from maintaining the status quo

This process allowed us to identify critical gaps, competing interests, and potential intervention points.

**The Marginalized Majority:**

At the core of Pittsburgh’s food insecurity crisis are those who are most vulnerable: [low-income African American and Latinx communities](https://healthyfoodaccess.org/why-hffi/), the elderly and disabled, and even local farmers struggling to stay afloat. For many, something as simple as buying fresh food is a challenge, with transportation barriers, financial strain, and government programs that fall short of meeting real needs. School meal programs attempt to fill the gap, but they often rely on highly processed foods that do little to support long-term health, reinforcing [dependence on the industrial food system](https://www.fns.usda.gov/data-research/data-visualization/school-foodservice-supply-chain-challenges-dashboard). Local farmers, who should be a cornerstone of a strong regional food network, instead face immense hurdles—corporate consolidation pushes them out of distribution channels, and logistical challenges make it difficult to get their food to the people who need it most. Even the land itself bears the weight of these systemic issues; Pittsburgh’s industrial legacy and reliance on extractive agricultural practices have left the [soil depleted](https://www.publicsource.org/toxic-metals-soil-pittsburgh-steel-emissions/) and waterways polluted, further limiting the potential for sustainable local food production.

**The Institutional Bystanders:**

On the other side of the equation are the institutions that hold immense influence over Pittsburgh’s economy (tech firms, universities, and medical centers) yet remain largely disconnected from the city’s food insecurity crisis. Universities like CMU and Duquesne, alongside corporate powerhouses like Google and Duolingo, have the resources to support a stronger local food system, whether through procurement policies, advocacy, or investment in sustainable food initiatives. ([CMU has started to exemplify healthy alternatives](https://www.pittsburghmagazine.com/you-dont-have-to-be-a-carnegie-mellon-university-student-to-enjoy-its-global-cuisine/)) However, their focus remains on profit and institutional expansion, leaving food justice issues on the periphery. Even healthcare institutions like UPMC, whose mission is rooted in public health, continue to rely on the [industrial food system](https://lifestylemedicine.org/articles/transforming-hospital-food/), where cost efficiency takes precedence [over nutritional value](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/1747-0080.12802). These institutions shape the city’s landscape and wield significant political and economic power, yet their engagement in food systems reform remains limited—an absence that reinforces the very inequities driving food insecurity.

**The Change Catalysts:**

Within this landscape, key stakeholders possess unique leverage to drive meaningful change. City planners and policymakers can reshape neighborhoods through zoning reforms that welcome grocery stores and [urban farms](https://www.hilltopurbanfarm.org/), while transit agencies can redesign routes to connect food-insecure communities with fresh food options. The academic sector brings crucial data and research capacity to identify effective solutions and inform policy decisions. And while food banks and grassroots organizations provide vital immediate support, their impact remains constrained without the [deeper structural changes](https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev-soc-031021-112747) these institutional players can enact. Through coordinated action, these stakeholders have the power to transform temporary assistance into lasting systemic solutions.

**The System Sustainers:**

On the opposing side are powerful actors whose business models are deeply intertwined with and profit from the persistence of food insecurity. ## **From Steel to Scarcity: How Braddock Became a Food Desert — and Fights to Feed Itself**

For nearly 150 years, Braddock has been a case study in how neighborhoods become food deserts and how communities fight back. In 1875, the Edgar Thomson Steel Works opened, pulling workers from farming into factory jobs, severing their direct connection to food production and making them dependent on industrial wages to eat (Niche). For a time, steel wealth sustained the town, but when deindustrialization hit in the 1980s, Braddock’s economy collapsed. Jobs vanished, families moved away, and businesses, including grocery stores, shuttered leaving behind empty storefronts and polluted land where fresh food could no longer grow (Landscape). What remained was a community without access to affordable, healthy food (Regime), trapped in a cycle of food insecurity shaped by its industrial past.

Today, Braddock remains deeply impacted by food insecurity, but resilience has emerged in response. Initiatives like [Braddock Farms](https://summerlee.house.gov/posts/congresswoman-summer-lee-celebrates-50000-grant-for-braddock-farms-highlights-urban-agricultures-role-in-fighting-food-insecurity), funded by the People’s Garden Initiative, and the [Braddock Youth Program’s Free Fridge](https://www.cbsnews.com/pittsburgh/news/local-groups-asking-for-donations-for-free-fridge-at-the-braddock-battlefield-history-center/) offer vital food access, carrying forward a long tradition of mutual aid within Black communities. These efforts are part of a lineage that stretches back to the networks built during slavery, evolving into culture-changing programs like the Black Panther Party’s free breakfast initiative, which laid the foundation for modern food banks, pantries, and community fridges. During COVID-19, free fridges appeared across the country as mutual aid once again became a lifeline in the face of economic instability. In Braddock, these grassroots efforts are more than survival strategies they are acts of defiance against systemic inequality, reclaiming food access in a neighborhood shaped by industrial decline. Neighborhoods like Hazelwood, Millvale, Swissvale, Homewood, and Garfield are following suit with urban agriculture initiatives like the [Hazelwood Initiative](https://www.hazelwoodinitiative.org/urban-ag-team) and [Millvale’s Food and Energy Hub](https://www.newsunrising.org/project/millvale-food-and-energy-hub/) to foster community resilience.

## **Driving Inequality: How Pittsburgh’s Roads Built Barriers to Food Access and Sparked a Fight for Change**

For over a century, Pittsburgh’s transportation infrastructure has shaped who has access to food and who goes hungry. When the internal combustion engine gained traction in the late 19th century, it set the stage for car dependency, reshaping cities around highways and personal vehicles (Landscape). Pittsburgh, with its steep hills and winding rivers, was already a challenging city to navigate, but the rapid expansion of the steel industry further entrenched car-centric development, sidelining public transit investment. For wealthier, predominantly white communities, suburban expansion and highways provided mobility; for lower-income and Black communities, transit became an afterthought, forcing many into food deserts.

By the 1960s, Pittsburgh’s urban renewal efforts, including the construction of the I-579 Expressway, physically severed the Hill District from downtown (Regime). This project displaced over 8,000 Black residents and 400 businesses, cutting off one of the city’s most culturally rich neighborhoods from economic opportunities and grocery access. Car ownership was assumed, but many residents couldn’t afford one. Instead of investing in robust transit solutions, policies continued to prioritize cars, leaving low-income communities stranded. Today, public transit remains a “wicked problem” in Pittsburgh as bus fare hikes disproportionately burden low-income riders[ (Pittsburgh for Public Transit)](https://www.pittsburghforpublictransit.org/new-fare-changes-leave-largest-inequities-in-place/), and transit deserts make grocery stores virtually unreachable for those without personal vehicles[ (Medium)](/td21-pgh-public-transportation/mapping-a-wicked-problem-lack-of-access-to-public-transit-in-pittsburgh-part-1-603ed816aec0).

But as in Braddock, resilience has emerged in response. Local initiatives like the Mobilify Southwest PA project are advocating for equitable transit policies and raising awareness of how transportation shapes food access[ (Mobilify)](https://mobilify.org/). New Sun Rising’s sponsorship program works to address the intersection of transit, housing, and hunger, supporting grassroots efforts that bring food closer to those who need it most[ (New Sun Rising)](https://www.newsunrising.org/the-blog-of-nsr/lack-of-affordable-housing-reliable-public-transit-tied-to-poverty-hunger-in-allegheny-county/). These initiatives are part of a long tradition of community-led solutions in Black neighborhoods, echoing the mutual aid networks that have historically filled the gaps left by policy failures. In a city shaped by highways, bridges, and transit neglect, these efforts push back against food insecurity not just by feeding people, but by demanding a more just and connected Pittsburgh.

## **Seeds of Injustice: Colonial Legacies and the Struggle for Food Sovereignty**

Food literacy is deeply tied to the history of colonialism, which disrupted [Indigenous food systems](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10153538/) and established patterns that still shape food access and health outcomes today. Before colonization, Indigenous communities in the Pittsburgh region thrived on reciprocal and regenerative food systems, such as the “Three Sisters” method , interplanting corn, beans, and squash to enrich soil and ensure food security. These systems fostered harmony with the land, staving off scarcity and promoting sustainable living. However, European settlers, driven by individualistic and capitalist mentalities that valued profit over human life, displaced native communities and destroyed these food systems, forcing them into dependency on external, colonial food sources also known as [“food colonialism”](https://www.theprojectheal.org/blog/food-colonialism). The seizure of land and the depletion of wildlife led to food insecurity and displacement, setting the stage for centuries of disempowerment and exclusion from access to healthy, culturally significant food.

Over time, European ideologies began to shape how food science and nutrition were understood, with education systems in the United States gradually adopting Westernized standards for anglo-centric diets to combat health challenges linked to industrialization and urbanization. By the early 1900s, initiatives like the Universal Free School Meals program sought to address child nutrition but often failed to provide healthy food, prioritizing cost over nutritional value (USDA). Similarly, [food literacy](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9614756/), as taught in schools, reflects these colonial perspectives centered on potentially [biased nutrition science](https://www.thefoodhistorian.com/blog/are-nutrition-science-and-nutritional-guidelines-racist) that predominantly addresses the needs of white communities while neglecting the [cultural relevance of Indigenous diets](https://www.eater.com/23981769/indigenous-food-health-colonization-climate-impact) and ancestral ingredients that are crucial to all diasporic populations.

The effects of this imbalance are still felt today, with nonwhite communities, particularly in cities like Pittsburgh, experiencing a disproportionate lack of access to healthy food, often in food deserts or, more accurately, food apartheid zones. Discriminatory urban planning and economic disinvestment have systematically excluded Black communities from food access, contributing to [higher rates of chronic diseases](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/ehpp/capstone-projects/final-racism-and-food-insecurity-a-public-health-crisis-2020.pdf) like obesity and cardiovascular conditions. However, there have been efforts to correct this imbalance, such as Alice Waters’[ Edible Schoolyard Project](https://edibleschoolyard.org/), which brought organic gardens to schools to increase access to fresh produce, and the potential policy shift in 2025 to reconsider SNAP’s allowance of “junk food” to address health disparities. This policy proposal is highly controversial, as many impacted SNAP recipients only have access to fast food or processed foods, meaning removing “junk food” from the program could leave them hungrier rather than healthier ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/)).

Today, the effects of colonization on food systems are clear: Indigenous practices were erased, and food systems became commercialized, prioritizing profit over people’s health. The colonial mindset embedded in food science still influences our food systems, and the legacy of this imbalance continues to contribute to food insecurity and poor health outcomes in marginalized communities.

## The Evolution of Food Insecurity in Pittsburgh: From Slavery to Modern-Day Challenges

The history of food insecurity in Pittsburgh is deeply intertwined with racial injustice and systemic inequalities, tracing back to the era of slavery. During this period, enslaved Africans were forced to create meals from scraps and foraged plants, laying the foundation for what would later become known as soul food. This culinary tradition, born out of necessity and resilience, traveled north with African Americans during the Great Migration (1916–1970). In Pittsburgh, soul food emerged as a symbol of cultural identity and a means of sustenance in the face of limited food access, reflecting the ongoing struggles with food insecurity in predominantly Black neighborhoods.

The legacy of slavery and systemic racism significantly impacted African Americans’ access to land and farming opportunities in Pittsburgh, forcing many into labor roles and exacerbating food insecurity. This cycle of poverty persists today, further compounded by the city’s history of redlining and economic disinvestment. These practices created food deserts in neighborhoods like the Hill District and Homewood, limiting access to affordable, nutritious food. Despite community-driven initiatives like the historical King’s Garden and modern organizations such as Grow Pittsburgh, which support urban farming and community gardens, food insecurity continues to disproportionately affect Black communities in the city. The persistence of these challenges underscores the need for ongoing efforts to address the deep-rooted racial and economic inequities that perpetuate food insecurity in Pittsburgh.

## Historical Roots and Climate Change: Exacerbating Food Insecurity in Pittsburgh

Pittsburgh’s legacy of industrial pollution and discriminatory urban planning has created a complex web of food insecurity, now intensified by climate change. The city’s history of steel production left a lasting environmental impact, while redlining policies of the 1930s segregated neighborhoods like the Hill District and Homewood. These areas became food deserts, with limited access to fresh, affordable produce due to a lack of supermarkets and an abundance of convenience stores. This historical disinvestment laid the groundwork for persistent nutritional inequities along racial and economic lines.

Climate change, partly fueled by Pittsburgh’s industrial past and ongoing urban emissions, now exacerbates food insecurity through multiple channels. Extreme weather events, such as more frequent storms and heatwaves, disrupt local food production and distribution networks. The city’s reliance on imported food becomes increasingly precarious as climate-induced weather patterns affect agriculture in distant regions, impacting food availability and costs. Moreover, the urban heat island effect, intensified by climate change, disproportionately affects low-income neighborhoods with less green space, making local food production more challenging.

The built environment, shaped by historical urban planning decisions, compounds these issues. Poor public transportation access to grocery stores, a result of car-centric development and neighborhood disinvestment, forces many residents to depend on less healthy, carbon-intensive food options. This perpetuates a cycle of poor health outcomes and environmental harm, as people rely more on processed foods with longer shelf lives and higher carbon footprints. As climate change worsens, it places additional stress on an already fragile food system in Pittsburgh’s most vulnerable neighborhoods, highlighting the urgent need for comprehensive solutions that address both historical inequities and current environmental challenges.

## **Conclusion**

Addressing food insecurity in Pittsburgh requires a deep historical perspective, as contemporary challenges are rooted in long-standing socio-economic transitions. Transition Design emphasizes the importance of understanding the historical origins of wicked problems to avoid repeating past mistakes and to guide sustainable, equitable solutions (Brand, 1999; Boulding, 1978). From the industrial boom of the early 20th century, which centralized food access within company-controlled economies, to mid-century suburbanization that exacerbated urban food deserts, Pittsburgh’s food landscape has been shaped by slow-moving yet powerful structural forces. Deindustrialization and economic decline in the late 20th century further entrenched food insecurity, as grocery chains abandoned low-income neighborhoods, and emergency food systems became stopgap solutions rather than systemic reforms.

Stewart Brand’s “pace layers” framework (1999) provides a useful lens for understanding the multi-speed nature of change in food systems. While rapid innovations such as urban agriculture, mobile markets, and food rescue initiatives offer immediate relief, slower-moving institutional barriers, including restrictive zoning laws, car-centric infrastructure, and corporate food monopolies, continue to maintain food inequities. As Brand suggests, a resilient society must balance fast-moving innovation with slower structural shifts, ensuring that emerging solutions are integrated into broader systemic change. Similarly, Elise Boulding (1978) warns against “temporal exhaustion,” arguing that modern society’s fixation on short-term crises prevents long-term thinking. This perspective is essential for tackling food insecurity, as sustainable solutions require a future-oriented approach that acknowledges deep-rooted historical patterns.

Historians like [R.G. Collingwood and Robert Crowcroft](https://www.historytoday.com/archive/feature/case-applied-history) argue that history provides an essential roadmap for understanding contemporary issues. Just as the trained woodsman can discern hidden dangers in the forest, historians recognize repeating patterns that, while not identical, often “rhyme” with the present (Richardson, 2020). The persistence of food insecurity in Pittsburgh mirrors past failures in public health infrastructure, urban planning, and economic policy. Learning from these patterns allows us to advocate for policies that move beyond emergency interventions toward systemic change, such as participatory food policy councils, local food hubs, and universal free meal programs.

By situating food insecurity within an expanded concept of time that looks 100 hundred years into the past and 100 hundred years into the future (Boulding, 1978) we can better understand the long-term consequences of policy decisions and economic trends. Transitioning to a just and sustainable food system requires not only responding to present-day crises but also reshaping the slow-moving structural forces that sustain inequity. Only by integrating historical insight with forward-thinking policy can Pittsburgh move toward lasting food security and resilience.

## Established Ways: What to Dismantle and What to Preserve

### What Needs to Transition Out:

Several established practices in Pittsburgh contribute significantly to persistent food insecurity and must be actively transitioned out. A critical step is decentralizing grocery stores, moving away from their current distant locations to neighborhood-based retailing, thus enhancing access to nutritious food. Additionally, addressing historical injustices stemming from practices such as redlining, urban renewal, and zoning policies is essential to correct entrenched inequalities. Shifting from corporate-dominated food supply chains towards decentralized local systems will empower communities and foster resilience. Lastly, it’s necessary to challenge cultural narratives that stigmatize food assistance, reframing food insecurity as a systemic issue rather than an individual shortcoming.

**Back-casting: **Raising Awareness and Challenging the Narrative

The first critical action is to raise awareness about food apartheid and systemic inequality. It is essential to shift the conversation from viewing food insecurity as an individual issue to recognizing it as a result of systemic structural inequality. This shift will help reshape public understanding and lay the foundation for addressing the root causes of food insecurity, rather than just its symptoms.

**Mid-casting: **Shifting Culture and Implementing Policy Change

Over the next few decades, cultural attitudes toward food assistance must undergo a transformation. The stigma surrounding seeking food assistance needs to be removed by reframing food insecurity as a systemic issue rather than a personal failing. This shift should be integrated into education systems, where curricula reflect the historical and ongoing realities of food injustice. Policy changes at the local, state, and national levels must align with this new cultural understanding, leading to sustainable and equitable food systems.

**Future-casting:** Localizing Food Systems and Empowering Communities

In 50–60 years, the reliance on SNAP should be phased out entirely as community-led solutions take over. Successful advocacy efforts must result in policies that ensure equitable access to culturally relevant, nutritious food for all. Localized initiatives, such as food cooperatives, urban farms, and food sovereignty projects, should replace government-run programs as the primary means of addressing food insecurity. These community-driven solutions will not only ensure access to healthy food but also foster resilience and empowerment within local communities. The government’s role would shift from managing programs like SNAP to supporting and sustaining these grassroots initiatives.

### What We Should Keep:

Several existing initiatives are successfully supporting Pittsburgh’s food justice and economic stability and should be maintained and expanded. Policies promoting urban agriculture through zoning laws have proven beneficial and should continue. Pittsburgh’s economic foundations in technology, higher education, and healthcare sectors remain vital for sustainable growth. Community-owned food co-ops effectively promote local self-sufficiency and food sovereignty and deserve ongoing support. Finally, government assistance programs such as SNAP, food banks, and community fridges are crucial safety nets that must remain robustly supported to ensure continued food security.

**Back-casting:** Shifting Cultural Norms and Expanding Access

A critical step is fostering a cultural shift that positions shopping at co-ops as a trendy and socially valued choice. As more people begin to prioritize local food systems and sustainability, there will be a growing demand for community-driven alternatives to corporate food retailers. However, during this phase, it’s essential to address the economic barriers that prevent low-income residents from fully participating in these systems, making co-ops and local food systems accessible to all, regardless of income.

**Mid-casting:** Policy Change and Institutionalizing Local Food Sovereignty

As cultural awareness and support for community-driven food systems grow, policy change will be necessary to institutionalize local food sovereignty. A new policy could mandate that every neighborhood actively contribute to local food systems by supporting urban agriculture, farmers’ markets, cooperative grocery stores, and culturally relevant food initiatives. This policy would prioritize equitable access to healthy food, ensure sustainability, and empower communities to make decisions about their food sources. By focusing on community-driven solutions, residents would gain greater control over their nutritional well-being and the resources that support it.

**Future-casting:** Community Wealth Building and the End of Corporate Ownership

In 50–60 years, community wealth-building initiatives and co-op models will take precedence over corporate control of local resources such as grocery stores, housing, and retail spaces. This shift will strengthen a localized economy, where the power and wealth generated by community-owned businesses circulate within the community rather than benefitting external corporate interests. Through cosmopolitan localism, these local economies will be interconnected with other community networks, fostering greater resilience and solidarity across regions.

## Emerging Ideas: Igniting the Transition

Innovative ideas and practices are reshaping Pittsburgh’s food systems and opening new pathways toward equitable food access. Significant efforts include food justice initiatives spearheaded by organizations like the Pittsburgh Food Policy Council and Urban Farms, actively redefining local food systems. Advances in farming technology, including year-round urban agriculture, have improved food availability, fostering local resilience. Mobile markets provide fresh produce directly to underserved areas, effectively addressing accessibility challenges. Educational initiatives such as 412 Food Rescue’s Cooking Matters program further empower communities with practical skills for affordable, nutritious cooking.

**Back-casting:** Expanding Mobile Markets with Access Challenges

In the near term, mobile markets will become a common practice to deliver fresh produce directly to food-deprived neighborhoods. These mobile markets will help address the immediate need for fresh food in underserved areas. However, challenges will persist, such as long lines at the mobile units and limited hours of operation, particularly for people who work late or have restrictive schedules. To address this, it will be necessary to explore ways to improve the logistics of these mobile markets and ensure that they are accessible to more people, at more convenient times.

**Mid-casting:** Delivering Free Food to Doorsteps

As the model for mobile markets evolves, a new policy or program will be established to ensure that food access reaches everyone, regardless of their ability to travel or their schedules. By this time, mobile markets will expand their services to deliver free food directly to people’s doorsteps. This service will target food-deprived neighborhoods and populations with specific access needs, including individuals with disabilities, aging residents, or those without reliable transportation. The focus will be on removing barriers to access, ensuring that healthy, fresh food reaches those who need it the most without requiring them to leave their homes.

**Future-casting: **Decentralized Local Food Systems

In 50–60 years, the food supply system will transition from a centralized, long-distance distribution model to a decentralized one centered around locally grown produce. Traditional grocery stores will no longer serve as the primary means of food access, as communities will rely on local farms, urban gardens, and decentralized food systems. This transformation will ensure that fresh, nutritious food is always accessible within the community without the need for extensive travel. Local food production and distribution networks will make it possible for everyone to have consistent access to healthy food, minimizing the environmental impact and maximizing the sustainability of the food system.

In the next 25–35 years, ethical and sustainable alliances will form between Indigenous harvesting practices and technological innovations. **Future-casting: **Shifting Focus to Conservation and Regeneration

In the next 50–60 years, the focus of food production will expand beyond simply growing food to include conserving local species, ecosystems, and cultural heritage. These hubs will not only produce food sustainably but also work toward preserving the natural balance of the land and protecting the cultural heritage that is intertwined with the landscape.

## Conclusion

The Living Land Vision is not a utopian fantasy; it is an urgent and achievable reorientation of our economies, relationships, and values. As we stand at the crossroads of escalating inequality and ecological collapse, the principles of Cosmopolitan Localism, Commoning, Mutual Aid, Pluriversality, and Manfred Max-Neef’s Theory of Needs offer the philosophical and practical foundation for transformation.

[**Cosmopolitan Localism**](https://transitiondesignseminarcmu.net/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/Cosmopolitan-Localism.pdf) guides us toward a future where communities thrive through locally rooted economies while remaining interconnected with global networks of solidarity and shared learning. By strengthening place-based food systems, knowledge-sharing, and governance, we resist corporate monopolies and cultivate self-sufficient, interdependent regions.

[**Commoning**](https://centerforneweconomics.org/publications/commoning-and-changemaking/) reminds us that essential resources like land, food, and knowledge are not commodities but shared inheritances that must be stewarded collectively. The Living Land Vision envisions a world where cooperative food networks, decentralized governance, and shared urban agriculture reshape our relationship with abundance, ensuring that no one is left behind.

[**Mutual Aid**](https://thisismold.com/series/relational-architectures/cooperation-jackson)** **challenges the false narrative of individualism by reaffirming that survival and flourishing are collective endeavors. In a society where food sovereignty is a birthright rather than a privilege, communities support one another not as an act of charity but as an extension of interdependence. Through time banking, solidarity economies, and reciprocal care, we dismantle the hierarchies that separate giver and receiver.

[**Pluriversality**](https://www.dukeupress.edu/designs-for-the-pluriverse) honors the multiplicity of worldviews, acknowledging that no single model can define a just and sustainable future. From Indigenous land stewardship to Black cooperative economics, from Pachakuti to Buen Vivir, from the Zapatistas to Gross National Happiness. This vision is not singular but a convergence of many wisdom traditions, each offering a vital thread in the tapestry of regenerative economies.

Finally, [**Manfred Max-Neef’s Theory of Needs**](http://www.ejolt.org/2012/12/needs/) provides a framework for redefining prosperity. Rather than centering economic growth, this vision prioritizes fundamental human needs: subsistence, protection, affection, understanding, participation, creation, identity, freedom, and transcendence. In a world shaped by the Living Land Vision, food systems are not just about feeding bodies but about sustaining dignity, culture, and community.

[**Transition Design**](https://transitiondesignseminarcmu.net/) offers a framework for actualizing this vision, moving from scarcity to stewardship and from charity to autonomy. Through backcasting, we charted the necessary steps to shift from corporate-dominated food systems to community-driven alternatives. Midcasting grounded these shifts in policy and cultural transformation, while forecasting prepared us for emerging challenges and opportunities. We identified what must be dismantled such as corporate control, systemic inequalities, and food insecurity stigma while reinforcing what must be preserved and expanded, including localized food systems, urban agriculture policies, and cooperative economies.

By integrating historical knowledge with emerging innovations, Pittsburgh and beyond can cultivate a future where food justice is not just an aspiration but a lived reality. The path forward is clear: a just, commoning-based future is not only possible, it is necessary. The seeds have already been planted. Now, it is up to us to cultivate them.
